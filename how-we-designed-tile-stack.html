How We Designed Tile Stack

The constraints, mistakes, and invisible decisions behind the game — straight from the developer.

By Rolin3 · September 16, 2026 · 6 min read

Tile Stack looks simple from the outside: a stack of tiles, seven slots, two tools. Most of the real design work went into decisions that are invisible until they are missing. This is the story of how the game got built — the constraints, the mistakes, and the reasoning behind the parts you never see.

Where the Game Came From

I built Tile Stack because I liked the tray-format tile games that went viral a few years ago, and I kept bouncing off their monetization. The two tools I actually needed — undo when I mis-tapped, shuffle when the board dried up — were exactly the two things those games sold back to me. The design brief wrote itself: keep the tension of the tray format, remove the tollbooths.

That brief produced the three pillars that never moved during development:

  1. Every level must be clearable without spending anything.
  2. Undo and Shuffle are unlimited and free.
  3. The game runs in the browser with no install and no account.

Why Seven Slots

The slot count is the single most consequential number in the game, and I tested it more than any other parameter. Five slots is brutal — the game punishes you for exploring the board at all. Nine slots makes most levels trivial; you can hoard singletons indefinitely and the slot system stops being a system.

Seven is the value where the pressure metric from slot management actually bites: you can hold two partial triples comfortably, three only briefly, and four never. If the game has a soul, it lives at seven.

The Level Generation Problem

The hard technical problem in this genre is guaranteeing that a generated board is solvable. Tile Stack uses a generate-then-verify pipeline: the board is built from a shuffled but legal removal order, so every level is, by construction, clearable. The Daily Challenge takes the same pipeline and seeds it from the date, which is why every player in the world gets the same challenge on a given day — and why yesterday's board cannot be replayed today.

One honest confession from playtesting: an early build generated boards purely randomly and then verified solvability by simulation. It worked, but the verification pass occasionally produced boards that were technically clearable and spiritually miserable — solvable only through perfect play. Tuning the generator to reject those boards, not just the unsolvable ones, doubled my playtesting time and halved player complaints when it shipped.

Designing Undo Around Human Fingers

On mobile, mis-taps are not an edge case; they are the majority case for Undo usage. Fingers are imprecise, screens are small, and tiles are adjacent. That is why Undo restores exactly one tap with no confirmation dialog — a confirm popup on every undo would have made the tool useless precisely when it was most needed. The cost of this choice is that accidental double undos can happen, and I accepted that trade-off deliberately. Fast forgiveness beats safe friction for a tool you reach for under mild frustration.

The BGM Decision

The 8-bit background music shipped in the first version, and the most common feedback for months was about audio: people loved it in short sessions and wanted it off during long ones — or during meetings they should not have been playing games in. The toggle was obvious; the subtler decision was to persist the preference locally, so the game respects your last choice instead of singing to you every session. It is a small thing, but "the game remembers my settings" is one of those quiet trust signals that separates a tool from a toy.

What I Would Build Differently

Every developer has a list. Mine, in order of regret:

  • A proper tutorial level. The early game teaches through play, and the how-to-play material lives here on the site (beginner guide) rather than in the game. In hindsight, two taught levels inside the game would have been worth it.
  • Level tagging. The campaign does not tell you which levels introduce which difficulty spikes. Players discover the structure themselves, which is charming once and confusing twice.
  • Statistics. People who love this game love counting things. The leaderboard scratches that itch partially — see the updates page for what is shipping next.

Building in public means the mistakes are permanent and the fixes are visible. That is the deal I signed up for, and honestly, it is what keeps me iterating.
