The History of Tile-Matching Games

Three centuries, four reinventions, one core skill. The family tree behind the stack you tap every day.

By Rolin3 · September 16, 2026 · 6 min read

Tile Stack belongs to a family of games that is older and stranger than most players realize. The "match three identical tiles to remove them" loop has been reinvented at least four times across three centuries, and each reinvention echoes in the games we play today. Here is that story, and where Tile Stack fits into it.

Before Screens: Mahjong Solitaire

The ancestor of every stacked-tile game is Mahjong Solitaire, a single-player tile game built from Chinese mahjong tiles. Instead of four players and betting, the solitaire variant spreads 144 tiles into layered layouts and asks one player to remove matching free tiles until the board is empty. It appeared as a computer game in the early 1980s — Shanghai (1986, Activision) is the version that carried it to a mass audience on early personal computers.

Two ideas from Mahjong Solitaire survive in virtually every modern tile matcher:

  • Layered boards where position gates access. You cannot take any tile, only tiles that are free — so the puzzle is about order of operations, not pattern spotting alone.
  • Board-clearing as the win condition. Not scoring, not racing — emptying the layout completely.

The Match-3 Explosion: Bejeweled and Candy Crush

The next reinvention swapped stacking for swapping. Bejeweled (2001, PopCap Games) established the modern "match-3" formula: a filled grid, adjacent swaps, triples vanishing, tiles falling in from above, chain reactions. It became one of the best-selling puzzle games ever made.

A decade later, Candy Crush Saga (2012, King) added level maps, boosters, and the free-to-play economy, and match-3 became the defining genre of mobile gaming. But notice what changed structurally: in the swapping branch, the board refills itself. The player never runs out of material — the pressure comes from move counts and objectives instead.

The Third Reinvention: The Sheep Game Wave

In 2022 and 2023, a Chinese mini-program game called Yang Le Ge Yang (羊了个羊, loosely "Sheep a Sheep") went massively viral, and with it a third structure spread worldwide: take tiles from a layered stack into a small visible tray, match triples in the tray, and lose if the tray fills. This is the structure Tile Stack uses.

The innovation was psychological as much as mechanical. By making the player's accumulating tiles permanently visible in the tray, the game turned slot management into visible, approaching doom. Every tap carries a small, legible risk. That tension is why the format exploded — and why it spread into dozens of clones and descendants across app stores and the web.

Where Tile Stack Sits in the Family Tree

Tile Stack is a direct descendant of the tray format, with three deliberate choices that distinguish it from the viral wave it grew out of:

  1. Deterministic fairness. Levels are hand-tuned so every board is clearable. There is no hidden "this one is unwinnable" roulette, which the hardest viral sheep-game levels were famous for.
  2. Recovery tools instead of paywalls. Undo and Shuffle are unlimited and free. The viral wave monetized exactly these two levers; Tile Stack treats them as part of the game's vocabulary.
  3. The browser as the platform. No install, no account. The game loads in a tab and plays in the space between other things — a nod to the solitaire-era origins of the genre, when games lived inside whatever computer you already had.

What the History Teaches About Playing Well

Every era of this genre rewards the same underlying skill: reading access order. Mahjong Solitaire players call it "reading free tiles." Match-3 players feel it as cascade planning. Tray-format players live it as slot management.
